What Is Axminster Carpet? A Guide for Hotels and Corporate Interiors

By Anchorline  ·  September 2026  ·  6 min read
← Back to Anchorline

Ask any hospitality designer what carpet goes in a five-star lobby and the answer comes back almost without thought: Axminster. It's specified so consistently, across so many categories of premium space, that it has become almost shorthand for "serious carpet." And yet a surprising number of designers and fit-out contractors can't explain exactly what makes it different from ordinary broadloom, or why it commands the price it does. The answer starts in a small English town in 1755.

270 Years of History

Axminster is not a brand. It's a construction method, named after the town in Devon, England where a weaver named Thomas Whitty developed it. Whitty had encountered Turkish and Persian carpets in London and was struck by their depth of pattern and structural integrity. He set about replicating that knotted-pile quality on English looms at commercial scale. What he created was a technique that locked pile yarns directly into the backing as the carpet was woven, row by row, without any secondary adhesive.

The fabric it produced was unlike anything that had existed in British manufacture: richly patterned, structurally sound, and capable of holding a complex design without the pile drifting or loosening over time. Within a few decades, Axminster carpet was being installed in royal residences and grand hotels across Europe. It earned Royal Warrants. It went into palaces. It crossed the Atlantic on ocean liners.

1755

Thomas Whitty founds the first Axminster carpet manufactory in Devon, England, developing the woven-pile construction that takes the town's name.

Late 1700s

Axminster carpet earns Royal Warrants and is installed in royal residences across Britain and Europe. The name becomes synonymous with a standard of quality that tufted manufacture hasn't yet arrived to challenge.

1800s

Industrial looms spread Axminster weaving well beyond Devon. Grand hotels, government buildings, ocean liners and embassies all specify it as a matter of course.

20th Century

The 80/20 wool-nylon blend is developed for commercial hospitality use, pairing wool's natural resilience with nylon's abrasion resistance. It becomes the industry standard and has stayed there.

Today

Axminster remains the benchmark specification for premium hotel lobbies, ballrooms, boardrooms and VIP suites globally. The construction principle is unchanged from what Whitty developed in 1755.

"The same construction that furnished royal palaces in the 18th century is still being specified for five-star hotel lobbies today. That's not nostalgia. That's a product that genuinely hasn't been bettered."

Woven, Not Tufted

Most carpet sold in India today is tufted. A tufting machine punches pile yarns through a pre-woven backing at speed, which is why tufted carpet is economical and widely available. The pile stays in place because an adhesive secondary backing holds it there, not because of anything structural in the fabric itself.

Axminster is different. The pile yarns are woven into the backing as the carpet is made, interlocked into the fabric structure row by row. There is no adhesive. The pile is part of the weave. This changes how the carpet behaves in almost every practical way.

Because the pile is mechanically secured rather than glued, individual tufts cannot work loose over time. In a high-footfall lobby or banquet corridor, that matters enormously. Tufted carpet in those conditions requires regular maintenance to manage tuft loss; Axminster doesn't have that problem. The woven structure also compresses and recovers differently from tufted pile, holding its height and surface integrity through years of heavy use rather than matting down in traffic lanes.

Pattern definition is another difference worth understanding. Axminster looms weave complex multi-colour designs row by row, with a precision that tufting cannot approach. That's why the classic hospitality carpet looks the way it does: sharp medallion repeats, clean geometric borders, colourways that register exactly. You cannot achieve the same result from a tufting machine regardless of how much you spend on it.

The 80/20 Blend

When specifiers write "Axminster, hospitality grade" on a schedule of finishes, they mean a specific yarn composition: 80% wool, 20% nylon. This blend has been the industry standard for decades and for good reason. Wool gives the carpet its natural resilience and the way it takes light, especially under the warm ambient lighting that defines good hotel design. Nylon adds abrasion resistance and extends the working life in areas where pure wool would eventually show wear.

Pure wool Axminster exists and it is beautiful, but it is a residential product. The 80/20 blend was developed specifically for commercial use, and in hospitality environments it is simply the right material.

Where It Gets Specified

Axminster is not a general-purpose carpet and no one uses it as one. It goes where the investment is justified by the visibility or the expected service life of the space. Hotel lobbies and reception areas are the most common application, because the lobby floor is one of the first things a guest registers and one of the last things they remember. Banquet halls and ballrooms work well because the scale of the space allows a complex pattern repeat to read properly. VIP suites and premium corridors benefit from the tactile quality of a wool pile underfoot in a way that synthetic broadloom simply does not deliver.

In corporate settings, the boardroom and executive floor are the natural locations. There is something about a woven carpet that reads differently to a guest or client than a tufted floor, even if they cannot say exactly what it is. The material signals a certain permanence and intention that off-the-shelf fit-out cannot replicate.

The Case for the Investment

Tufted broadloom is entirely the right product for standard guest rooms, back-of-house corridors, and projects working within a tight budget. It is cost-effective, quick to source, and available in enough construction weights to handle almost any application. There is no reason to specify Axminster where standard broadloom will do the job properly.

But in the spaces where Axminster is appropriate, the investment argument is actually quite straightforward. A well-specified woven carpet will outlast two or three replacement cycles of a cheaper tufted product in the same location. Over a ten-year hotel ownership period, the total cost of ownership frequently favours Axminster, even before you account for the disruption and associated costs of those replacement cycles. Projects that choose on initial price alone sometimes end up spending more in the end.

Then there is the design dimension. Standard broadloom means selecting from an existing collection. Axminster means designing from scratch if you want to: your own medallion, your own colourway, a border that coordinates with the FF&E palette, a pattern scaled to the proportions of a specific corridor or ballroom. For a hotel with a genuine design identity rather than an off-the-shelf interior, that distinction matters.

Custom Design and the Specification Process

The design process for a bespoke Axminster project typically begins with artwork, either supplied by the designer or developed collaboratively. Colourways are confirmed from physical yarn samples before production starts, and the carpet is woven to room dimensions rather than cut from a roll. Lead times are longer than stock broadloom, usually in the 12 to 20 week range from artwork sign-off, but the end result is a floor that belongs to that project and no other.

Most designers who have specified bespoke Axminster once continue to do so. The process is not complicated once you know how it works, and the result is genuinely hard to achieve by any other means.
