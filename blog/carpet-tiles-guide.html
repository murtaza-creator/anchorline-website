Carpet Tile for Offices and Hotels: GSM, Backing and the Case for Modular Replacement

By Anchorline  ·  September 2026  ·  7 min read
← Back to Anchorline

A coffee spill in an open-plan office. A chair-caster wear pattern worn into the carpet in front of a busy workstation. A duct access panel under the floor that needs to come up every time the IT team makes a change. These are not unusual problems. They are the everyday realities of commercial flooring, and broadloom carpet handles all of them badly. Carpet tile exists specifically because those problems are common enough to warrant a different product.

Understanding carpet tile properly means understanding the problems it was designed to solve. Start there and the specification decisions that follow become considerably clearer.

What Carpet Tile Actually Is

Carpet tile is modular carpet, manufactured in squares, typically 50cm x 50cm or 60cm x 60cm. The pile surface is the same material you would find in a broadloom product: tufted fibre, usually nylon or a nylon-wool blend, cut to a pile height suited to the weight of use. What distinguishes a carpet tile from a section of broadloom is the backing system and the construction that gives each tile dimensional stability.

A carpet tile's backing is typically a bitumen or PVC compound, applied thick enough to give the tile rigidity and weight. That rigidity is the whole point. A carpet tile holds its shape because it has to: it needs to sit flat against an access floor panel, it needs to align precisely with adjacent tiles, and it needs to stay put when someone walks across it without an adhesive backing holding it in place. The construction is engineered around those requirements in a way that broadloom never is.

Installation is either fully adhered using a pressure-sensitive adhesive, or loose-lay on access floor panels designed to accept tiles without bonding. Both methods are reversible in a way that broadloom never is, which is the first reason facilities managers tend to specify tile once they have managed a broadloom installation through a single major repair.

GSM: Why It Matters and What the Numbers Mean

GSM stands for grams per square metre. It measures the weight of the pile layer, and it is the single most useful number for comparing carpet tiles across products and price points. Everything else, pile height, fibre type, backing weight, can be the same between two tiles and a significant difference in GSM will still determine how they perform over time.

A heavier pile packs more fibre into the same surface area. That means more resilience when pile is compressed underfoot, slower degradation of the surface texture, and better recovery of pile height in traffic lanes. In practical terms, a carpet tile with a pile weight of 600 GSM and one with 900 GSM might look identical when new. Three years into a busy commercial installation, they will not.

For general commercial office use, a pile weight in the range of 500 to 650 GSM is the normal specification. For higher-footfall applications, entrance areas, hotel corridors, conference suites, that range shifts upward: 700 to 950 GSM is where most hospitality-grade tiles sit. Heavy-duty entrance tiles in locations like hotel lobbies or transport facilities sometimes exceed 1000 GSM.

The relationship between GSM and price is approximately linear. You pay more for more pile, and in a commercial application the additional cost is almost always recovered through extended replacement cycles. The mistake most project managers make is specifying on surface appearance rather than pile weight, which means the product looks right at installation and fails prematurely in use.

"GSM is the number to ask for before everything else. Pile height and colour you can see. Pile weight you cannot, and it is the number that determines how long the product lasts."

FR Certification and Why Specifiers Ask for It

FR stands for flame retardant. In commercial and hospitality projects, building regulations and project insurers will typically require carpet to meet a defined fire performance standard, and the applicable standard varies by geography, use type, and the floor level of the installation.

The most commonly referenced standards in Indian commercial projects are IS 11871 (the Bureau of Indian Standards flame spread classification) and international standards such as EN 13501-1 (European fire classification) which appears frequently in specifications written by international hotel brands or architects working to global standards.

FR-certified carpet tile is not a fundamentally different product. It is tile that has been manufactured with fibre and backing materials that meet defined thresholds for flame spread, smoke production, and heat release, and that has been tested and certified accordingly. In practical terms, most commercial-grade nylon tile from reputable manufacturers carries some form of FR certification as standard, because the commercial market demands it.

What matters from a specification standpoint is confirming that the certification the supplier claims applies to the product as supplied, not just the pile fibre in isolation. Backing materials contribute significantly to fire performance, and a tile whose pile meets an FR threshold but whose backing does not may fail the overall test. Ask for the test certificate, not just the claim.

The Replaceability Advantage

This is the argument that closes most conversations about carpet tile in commercial contexts. A broadloom installation is a single piece of fabric covering a floor. When one area of it fails, whether through staining, wear, or physical damage, you have three choices: live with it, patch it (which is always visible), or replace the entire installation. None of those options is good.

A carpet tile installation is a field of individual modules. When one tile fails, you replace that tile. The work takes minutes, the cost is negligible compared to a full refurbishment, and if you ordered extra tiles at installation time for exactly this purpose, the replacement is invisible because it matches exactly. This is not a theoretical advantage. It is the reason facilities-managed corporate buildings almost universally specify tile rather than broadloom for their office floors.

The logic extends to phased fit-outs and growing organisations. A tenant taking additional floors can tile their new space in a product that matches existing floors and continue to do so as they expand. Broadloom cannot offer that. The tile specification acts as a long-term flooring standard rather than a one-time installation decision.

Where Carpet Tile Gets Specified

The primary market for carpet tile is commercial office space. Open-plan floors, cellular offices, corridors, meeting rooms, and reception areas in commercial buildings are the default application, and carpet tile dominates this market precisely because of the access floor compatibility and replacement logic described above. Any building with a raised access floor and a business occupancy is a natural carpet tile candidate.

Hospitality applications are a distinct category and require a different approach to specification. Hotels use carpet tile in back-of-house corridors, function rooms that double as exhibition spaces, conference suites requiring regular reconfiguration, and ground-floor areas subject to heavy pedestrian traffic and maintenance access. The product category for these applications sits at the heavier end of the GSM range and typically carries a more conservative colourway than the patterned tiles common in corporate fit-outs.

For hotel guest rooms and lobby areas, carpet tile is generally not the right product. Guest rooms warrant broadloom for the seamless, soft underfoot experience that defines quality in that context. Lobbies, where Axminster is the premium specification, are not a carpet tile environment either. The right product in the right place is the principle: tile where the operational advantages outweigh the aesthetic difference, broadloom or woven carpet where the experience of the space is the priority.

What to Ask Before You Specify

The questions worth asking any carpet tile supplier before committing to a specification:

  • What is the pile weight in GSM? Not pile height, not fibre description. The actual grams per square metre figure.
  • What FR certification does the product carry? And does that certification apply to the tile as a complete assembly, not just the pile fibre?
  • What is the backing construction? Bitumen, PVC, or a cushioned secondary backing all perform differently under access floor conditions. Know which you are getting.
  • Is stock held in India or is it imported to order? This affects lead time and the feasibility of ordering additional tiles from the same batch for future replacement.
  • What is the minimum order quantity? For a facilities replacement strategy to work, you need to be able to order small quantities at matching specifications over the life of the installation.

A supplier who cannot answer these questions clearly is a supplier who does not know their own product well enough for a commercial specification.
