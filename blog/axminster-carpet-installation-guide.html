How Axminster Carpet Is Installed: What Hotels and Fit-Out Teams Should Know

By Anchorline  ·  October 2026  ·  7 min read
← Back to Anchorline

Specifying genuine woven Axminster is only half the decision. How it goes down on site determines whether that investment performs for the fifteen or twenty years it is supposed to, or starts showing seams and edge lift within eighteen months. Hotels and corporate fit-outs lose more carpet life to installation shortcuts than to the carpet itself, and it rarely shows up until the first inspection after handover — by which point the installer has moved on and the liability sits with whoever signed off the job.

This is the part of the specification that rarely makes it onto a schedule of finishes, but it should. Here is what actually happens between the carpet arriving on site and a guest walking across it. For a broader introduction to what Axminster carpet actually is and how it is constructed, see What Is Axminster Carpet? A Guide for Hotels and Corporate Interiors.

Direct Stick vs. Double Stick

There are two accepted ways to fix broadloom carpet to a commercial subfloor, and the choice is not a matter of preference.

Direct stick bonds the carpet straight to the subfloor with adhesive. It is faster and cheaper, and it works well in lower-traffic areas where the floor underneath is stable and unlikely to move. What it does not do is absorb impact or hide minor subfloor irregularities — every dip, seam or trowel mark in the concrete below eventually telegraphs through to the surface.

Double stick adds a cushion underlay bonded to the subfloor first, with the carpet then bonded to that underlay. This is the method manufacturers specify for woven Axminster in hotel corridors, lobbies and other high-traffic commercial spaces, for three reasons: the underlay absorbs foot traffic and extends pile life, it masks subfloor imperfections that would otherwise show through a woven face, and it meets the installation standard most carpet warranties are written against. Skip it, and a manufacturer can and will decline a warranty claim on premature wear.

"For a hospitality project specifying genuine Axminster, double stick with the manufacturer's recommended underlay is not an upgrade. It is the baseline."

The underlay itself matters too. Commercial Axminster requires a firm, dense cushion — bonded rubber or dense frothed polyurethane foam are the commercial norm. The soft rebond foam common in residential carpet installations should not be used under woven commercial Axminster; it is too compressible and can cause the carpet to move and ripple under sustained traffic. Adhesive is applied with a notched trowel at the specified notch size on both surfaces — subfloor to underlay, underlay to carpet — to ensure even, controlled coverage throughout.

What Has to Happen Before the Carpet Is Laid

Most installation problems trace back to the subfloor, not the carpet. Before any adhesive goes down, a competent installer checks three things.

Subfloor condition. The concrete or screed needs to be clean, level and structurally sound — free of loose debris, curing compound residue and surface cracks that could telegraph through a woven pile. Minor unevenness gets levelled with a self-levelling compound before anything else happens.

Moisture testing. Concrete that has not fully cured, or that sits over a slab without an adequate damp-proof membrane, will trap moisture against the underside of the carpet. That moisture has nowhere to go and eventually shows up as adhesive failure, odour or mould — problems that surface months after handover, not during installation. A calcium chloride or relative humidity test against the adhesive manufacturer's threshold is standard practice on any commercial job, and it is worth insisting on in writing.

Acclimatisation. Carpet rolls that have travelled any distance — especially by air or across a significant temperature change — need time in the installation environment before they are cut and laid. This lets the material settle to the ambient temperature and humidity of the room, which keeps the dimensions stable during and after fitting.

None of this is visible in the finished floor. All of it determines whether that floor still looks right in year five.

Seaming and Pattern Matching

Axminster's woven construction is precise enough to carry a complex multi-colour design, which makes a bad seam far more obvious than it would be on a plain-pile tufted floor. A corridor running a medallion or border repeat only reads correctly if every seam lines the pattern up exactly — a few millimetres off and the eye catches it immediately, every time someone walks the length of it.

Getting this right starts with a seaming diagram — what the trade calls a "flooded floor plan" — drawn up before any cutting happens, working out seam placement against the pattern repeat and the room's traffic lines so joins fall where they are least visible. Breadths are then dry-laid against that plan and checked against a central reference line, kept matched and aligned in both length and width, before anything is bonded. Only once the pattern is confirmed correct is the carpet glided flat against the underlay to work out every air pocket and committed to adhesive.

There is a detail here that catches projects out even when the team is experienced: individual Axminster rolls can weave to very slightly different finished lengths or pattern dimensions within the same production run. All rolls for a contiguous corridor or room are therefore dry-laid and compared before cutting, and the longest — the "key" roll — is installed first as the reference every other roll is stretched and matched to. Installing a shorter roll first can make a full pattern match impossible later.

This is where installer experience with woven carpet specifically matters most. A team used to tufted broadloom can fit a straight seam without much difficulty; matching a repeating pattern across a 40-metre corridor run is a different skill, and it is usually the first thing that goes wrong on a job given to a general flooring contractor rather than one with genuine Axminster experience.

Why the Installer Matters as Much as the Carpet

Most woven Axminster carries a manufacturer's warranty against premature wear, pile loss and construction defects — but that warranty is conditional on the carpet being installed to the manufacturer's specification. Double-stick method, correct underlay grade, moisture testing completed, adhesive coverage rates met: all of it is usually named explicitly in the warranty terms, and a manufacturer's technical team can tell from a site inspection whether those conditions were met.

Manufacturer guidance for commercial Axminster also specifies a minimum experience bar for installers — typically several years of work specifically with woven patterned carpet, at journeyman or master-certified level. This is specialist work, not general flooring installation. The difference is audible in a warranty dispute.

This is why genuine Axminster projects are costed and installed through the manufacturer's own certified installation teams, or contractors formally approved to their standard, rather than handed to whichever flooring crew is already on site for the rest of the fit-out. It costs more than a generalist quote. It is also the only way the warranty stays intact if something does go wrong five years in, and the only way the floor performs the way the specification assumed it would.

Sequencing It Into the Project Timeline

Installation is usually the last trade into a space, and it needs the room handed over substantially complete — painting, ceiling work and any wet trades finished, so dust and moisture are no longer a risk to the adhesive bond. A typical sequence for a hotel corridor or lobby runs:

  1. Subfloor handover and inspection — moisture testing and levelling, 1–2 days depending on condition.
  2. Acclimatisation — carpet rolls rest on site, typically 24–48 hours.
  3. Underlay installation (double stick) — bonded and left to cure per adhesive instructions.
  4. Layout, cutting and seaming — pattern match confirmed before final bonding.
  5. Final bond and inspection — full room walked and checked against the approved sample.

Built into a fit-out programme with reasonable float, this adds up to a matter of days per room or corridor section, not weeks — but it needs to be on the programme as its own line item, not folded into "flooring" as an afterthought the week before opening.

One practical detail worth flagging to the broader project team: freshly installed Axminster should never be covered with plastic sheeting while other trades finish around it. Plastic traps moisture against the pile and can void the standard warranty outright — it is a contractual condition on most manufacturer warranties, not just a best-practice tip. Plywood or heavy-duty craft paper protects the floor just as well without the risk, and it is a cheap instruction to miss if nobody on site knows to give it.
