LVT Flooring for Commercial Spaces: Waterproofing, Wear Layers and When It Beats Carpet

By Anchorline  ·  September 2026  ·  7 min read
← Back to Anchorline

Most commercial flooring briefs default to carpet. It is the familiar spec — soft, acoustically comfortable, visually warm — and project teams often carry it into spaces where it is not actually the right choice. A hotel corridor with twice-daily trolley traffic. A reception area next to a wet zone. A spa changing room where moisture is a constant. In each of those spaces, carpet will perform below expectations and require replacement sooner than the budget planned for.

The question is not whether LVT is better than carpet as a category. It is not. The question is whether the specific space you are specifying is one where LVT's actual technical advantages — waterproofing, wear resistance, cleaning speed, performance under rolling loads — outweigh the acoustic and comfort properties that carpet provides. This guide is about making that call clearly, without overselling either material.

What LVT Actually Is

Luxury Vinyl Tile is a multi-layer construction. At its core is a rigid or semi-rigid base layer that gives the plank or tile its dimensional stability. Above that sits a printed decorative layer — the wood grain, stone, or texture visual — which is what you see. On top of everything is a clear wear layer: the transparent coating that protects the decorative surface from scuffing, scratching, and UV fading. That wear layer is the single most important number to ask a supplier for, and we return to it below.

It is worth naming what LVT is not. Budget sheet vinyl — the material that peels at the edges and yellows in sunlight — is a fundamentally different product. The rigid core and proper wear layer are what distinguish commercial-grade LVT from that category, and conflating them is one of the more common specification mistakes in commercial interiors.

On installation: LVT comes in two main formats. Click-lock planks or tiles — as used across Anchorline's current range — interlock without adhesive, which makes installation faster and, critically, makes individual plank replacement possible if a section is damaged. Glue-down LVT is more common in very high-traffic commercial applications where dimensional stability under rolling loads is the priority. For most hospitality and commercial interiors, click-lock performs well and offers a meaningful maintenance advantage.

Waterproofing: Why It Actually Matters in a Commercial Context

100% waterproof construction means the plank will not absorb moisture, swell, warp, or develop mould regardless of what is spilled on it or how humid the ambient environment is. That is not a marginal benefit in commercial use — it is the specification decision that determines whether a floor lasts five years or fifteen in certain environments.

The use cases where this matters most:

  • Hotel bathrooms and wet-zone adjacencies. Flooring immediately outside wet rooms, in spa changing areas, and in any space where wet feet are a regular occurrence is a waterproofing problem first and an aesthetic problem second.
  • F&B and kitchen-adjacent areas. Service corridors, staff areas, and floors near kitchen pass-throughs see liquid spills consistently. Carpet in these zones traps moisture and develops odour regardless of cleaning frequency — a problem that gets worse, not better, over time.
  • Basement and ground-floor installations. Any floor directly on or below grade is exposed to ambient moisture from the slab. Waterproof LVT is appropriate here; carpet over a damp slab is not.
  • Coastal and high-humidity locations. Projects in Mumbai, coastal Kerala, or any high-humidity climate see ambient moisture levels that accelerate the degradation of flooring that is not fully sealed. LVT is inherently suited to these environments in a way that broadloom carpet is not.

"Carpet in wet or moisture-prone zones traps moisture and odour over time regardless of underlay quality. That is a maintenance problem that compounds — it does not resolve with better cleaning."

Wear Layer and UV Stability: What Actually Determines Lifespan

The wear layer is the transparent coating on the surface of the LVT plank. It is what resists scuffing from foot traffic, scratching from chair legs and trolley wheels, and fading from sunlight. Two LVT products can look identical in a sample room and perform completely differently over three years of commercial use — the difference is almost always in the wear layer.

What to ask for: wear layer thickness in millimetres, not just "commercial grade" or "heavy duty." Thicker wear layers are appropriate for heavier traffic; thinner wear layers are appropriate for low-traffic areas where cost is a priority. Do not specify a wear layer thinner than what your traffic level demands, and do not pay for a specification heavier than you need. Ask Anchorline for the current range's wear layer specifications before finalising any brief — we do not publish specific figures here because product ranges are updated periodically.

UV stability matters wherever the floor receives direct sunlight — lobbies with full-height glazing, poolside interiors, reception areas with south-facing windows. LVT without a UV-stable finish will visibly yellow or fade in these applications within 18 to 24 months. The current Anchorline range specifies UV-stable finish across all six products precisely because it is a commercial specification requirement, not a premium add-on.

Anti-slip texture is sometimes presented as a purely aesthetic finish but it is a safety specification in commercial flooring. Any wet-zone or high-traffic public area has a duty-of-care dimension that makes anti-slip surface treatment a compliance consideration, not just a preference.

Hotel corridors and back-of-house

  • Daily trolley, linen cart and room-service traffic
  • Needs to clean quickly between turns
  • Rolling loads permanently compress carpet pile over 12–18 months
LVT is the better spec

Wet zones and moisture-exposed areas

  • Spa changing rooms, bathroom adjacencies
  • F&B service areas and kitchen corridors
  • Poolside-adjacent interiors, coastal properties

  • UV-stable finish handles glazed lobby light exposure

Where Carpet Still Wins

This section exists because the honest answer to "LVT or carpet?" is not always LVT, and a guide that oversells one material is not a useful guide.

Carpet is the better spec

Guest room bedrooms

  • Acoustic dampening is part of the guest experience
  • Underfoot warmth and comfort on first contact
  • Low rolling traffic — LVT's durability advantage does not apply
Carpet is the better spec

Boardrooms and meeting rooms

  • Acoustic performance is a functional requirement
  • Soft furnishing environments where hard-surface echo is noticeable
  • Custom Axminster or broadloom can carry a design identity LVT cannot

LVT is a hard surface. Footfall is audible on it, it does not absorb ambient sound, and it does not have the tactile warmth that carpet delivers underfoot. In any space where those properties matter to the occupant experience — and in hospitality, they matter in bedrooms above all — carpet remains the right specification. The goal is not to replace carpet everywhere. It is to stop specifying carpet in spaces where it will fail.
