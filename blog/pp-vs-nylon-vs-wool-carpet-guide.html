PP Carpet vs. Nylon vs. Wool: A Buyer's Guide for Commercial Projects on a Budget

By Anchorline  ·  September 2026  ·  7 min read
← Back to Anchorline

Every commercial flooring conversation eventually arrives at the same tension: budget is real, but the cheapest option on a per-square-foot basis is not always the cheapest option once you account for replacement cycles and the cost of a floor that looks worn before the tenancy ends. Nowhere is this more apparent than in fibre choice.

The three fibres that appear in almost every commercial carpet specification, at every price point, are polypropylene (PP), nylon, and wool. They are not interchangeable. They serve different use cases, perform differently under the same traffic conditions, and make sense for different types of projects. Understanding what actually separates them, rather than just comparing price lists, is where most procurement decisions should start.

PP (Polypropylene): The Budget Tier

Budget tier

Polypropylene is a synthetic fibre manufactured through an extrusion process. The property that defines it commercially is solution dyeing: the colour is added to the polymer melt before the fibre is formed, which means the colour runs through the full cross-section of each fibre strand rather than being applied to the surface after production. This is why PP carpet resists fading and staining as well as it does. A bleach spill, UV exposure from a glazed facade, or chemical cleaning product that would strip surface dye from a tufted nylon carpet does considerably less damage to a solution-dyed PP fibre.

PP is available in both broadloom and modular tile formats. It takes colour well across a wide palette, and the solution-dyeing process means that colour consistency across large orders is reliable in a way that surface-dyed alternatives are not. For brand-colour carpet tiles installed under strong commercial or retail lighting, those properties matter more than they might first appear.

The cost per square foot is the lowest of the three fibres covered here, by a significant margin. For large-area installations where budget is the primary constraint, that difference is decisive.

"The honest limitation of PP is resilience under sustained heavy traffic. The fibre has lower elastic recovery than nylon, which means it flattens more readily in high-footfall zones and recovers pile height more slowly. Under light to moderate use, this is rarely visible. Under heavy event or office traffic, it becomes apparent within a few years."

Nylon: The Commercial Mid-Tier

Mid tier

Nylon is the workhorse of commercial carpet. It sits at a higher cost per square foot than PP but below wool, and its performance credentials under sustained heavy traffic justify that middle position in almost every commercial application.

The key property that separates nylon from PP in high-footfall environments is elastic recovery. Nylon fibre has a significantly higher resilience, meaning pile that is compressed underfoot recovers its height more readily when pressure is released. In a busy office corridor, a hotel lobby corridor, or a conference suite that takes event traffic, this difference is visible in the surface appearance of the carpet after two or three years of use. A nylon installation in those conditions maintains a cleaner, less matted surface than PP at the same GSM.

Nylon carpet is specified in two pile constructions: cut pile, where each tuft is sheared to a level, velvet-like surface, and loop pile, where the yarn is left uncut, forming a looped surface that is denser underfoot and shows footprints and vacuum marks less than cut pile. Loop pile is the more common choice for high-traffic commercial corridors and back-of-house areas because the uncut loops resist crushing and matting better than cut pile at the same GSM. Anchorline supplies both constructions in nylon broadloom and carpet tile — ask for loop pile specifically if the space is heavy-traffic and low-maintenance is the priority.

Nylon is the standard fibre for most commercial carpet tile in India. It accepts surface dyeing and solution dyeing, giving manufacturers a wider range of colour and pattern options than PP. FR-certified nylon tile is widely available, which is why it dominates commercial fit-out specifications where building regulations require documented fire performance.

For the majority of commercial office floors, hotel back-of-house areas, and hospitality function spaces, nylon at the right GSM is the correct default. It is not the cheapest option and it is not the most durable, but it sits at the intersection of cost and performance that makes sense for the widest range of commercial applications.

For more on GSM, backing types, and how to specify nylon carpet tile correctly, see Carpet Tile for Offices and Hotels: GSM, Backing and the Case for Modular Replacement.

Wool and Wool Blends: The Premium Tier

Premium tier

Wool sits at the top of the cost-per-square-foot range and is used in a narrow but clearly defined set of applications: hotel lobbies, boardrooms, VIP suites, ballrooms, and any space where the appearance of the floor is commercially important over a 10-year horizon.

The properties that justify the premium are natural resilience, the way wool takes dye (which gives it a depth of colour and variation in the pile surface that synthetic fibres cannot replicate under lighting), and the tactile quality underfoot that distinguishes a wool floor from a synthetic one in a guest-facing space. Wool also has natural moisture management properties that perform well in the Indian climate.

In commercial hospitality applications, wool is almost always used in a blend rather than as a pure fibre. The standard specification is 80% wool and 20% nylon: the wool provides the appearance, resilience, and feel, and the nylon addition increases abrasion resistance for commercial traffic levels. This blend, used in woven Axminster construction, has been the benchmark for hotel carpet specification for decades.

Where PP Genuinely Makes Sense

PP is not a compromise product used when nothing better is available. There are applications where it is the correct specification, and understanding those applications means you get the right product rather than an oversized budget allocation for a space that doesn't need it.

  • Back-of-house and staff areas. Hotel back-of-house corridors, staff rooms, service passageways, and storage areas do not carry guest-facing traffic and do not require the durability or appearance retention that justifies nylon's additional cost. PP at a commercial GSM is the right product here.
  • Short-lease commercial fit-outs. If a commercial tenant is in a space for three years, with no expectation of a long-term tenancy, specifying a 10-year carpet is not the right financial decision. PP's lower upfront cost aligns with a shorter value horizon.
  • Projects with a planned refresh cycle. Some operators refresh their interior on a defined schedule regardless of the condition of the existing installation. For those projects, PP's lower lifespan is built into the model rather than being a limitation.
  • Brand-colour and high-saturation applications. Where the specification calls for a specific brand colour or a high-saturation design under strong retail or commercial lighting, PP's solution-dyed fade resistance is a genuine technical advantage over surface-dyed alternatives. The colour will stay true under UV exposure and cleaning cycles in a way that surface-dyed fibre may not.

Where PP Is the Wrong Call

The point of this section is not to discourage PP but to be accurate about where it performs below expectations, because a mismatch between product and application is more expensive in the long run than the cost difference between fibres.

  • High-traffic lobbies and reception areas that carry sustained footfall through the working day. The pile flattening that PP develops under those conditions becomes visible faster than most operators expect, and it is not reversible through standard maintenance.
  • Banquet halls and ballrooms with heavy event turnover, spike heel traffic, and repeated deep cleaning. The combination of physical compression and aggressive cleaning cycles degrades PP pile faster than nylon or wool in the same conditions.
  • Guest-facing flagship spaces where the appearance of the floor reflects on the quality of the venue. A worn PP floor in a hotel lobby or restaurant entrance reads differently to a guest than a worn nylon floor in the same location, because the pile flattening is more visually pronounced.
  • Any project where the carpet is expected to last 8 to 10 years under moderate-to-heavy use without a mid-life replacement. PP is not rated for that combination of lifespan and traffic level. Nylon or wool is the honest specification for that brief.
